Do Billing Companies Need a BAA? HIPAA Rules Explained

ClaimPaid · October 24, 2026 · 6 min read
The short answer

Yes. Any billing company — or RCM software vendor — that creates, receives, maintains, or transmits protected health information (PHI) on your behalf is a HIPAA business associate and must sign a business associate agreement (BAA) with you. No BAA, no PHI access: the covered entity is responsible for the gap, and OCR enforcement treats a missing BAA as a basic compliance failure.

Every billing relationship touches PHI — patient names, dates of service, diagnoses, insurance IDs flow through claims, remits, and worklists daily. Under HIPAA, any vendor touching that data on your behalf is your business associate, and the law requires a written business associate agreement before they touch a single record. This isn't optional paperwork; it's one of the first things OCR asks for in an investigation.

Who counts as a business associate

A business associate is any person or company that creates, receives, maintains, or transmits PHI on behalf of a covered entity. That plainly includes:

  • Billing companies and RCM services — they live in your PHI all day.
  • RCM and practice management software vendors — including cloud-hosted systems where the vendor can access the data.
  • Clearinghouses — they translate and route your PHI-bearing claim files.
  • AI and analytics vendors — any tool that processes claim or clinical data, including AI coding assistants, is handling PHI.

The test is access, not intent: if the vendor can access PHI in the course of the work, they're a business associate. "We never look at it" isn't a defense.

"No BAA, no PHI access" — the covered entity owns the compliance gap when the agreement is missing, not just the vendor.

What the BAA must cover

HIPAA specifies the required elements. Your BAA should address:

  • Permitted uses and disclosures — the vendor may use PHI only as the agreement allows, typically to perform the contracted billing functions.
  • Safeguards — administrative, physical, and technical protections for ePHI.
  • Breach notification — the vendor must report breaches and security incidents to you promptly so you can meet your own notification duties.
  • Subcontractor flow-down — the vendor's subcontractors that touch PHI need BAAs with the vendor, with equivalent protections.
  • Return or destruction — what happens to PHI when the relationship ends.
  • Compliance with the Security Rule — business associates are directly liable for Security Rule failures.

Red flags in vendor contracts

  • No BAA offered. A billing vendor that won't sign a BAA is disqualifying — full stop.
  • "We're just a conduit." The conduit exception is narrow (think postal service). A billing company that stores, processes, or works your claims is not a conduit.
  • Vague breach-notification terms. "Reasonable efforts" isn't a timeline. You need defined notification windows.
  • Data-use overreach. Watch for clauses letting the vendor use your de-identified or aggregate data beyond the contracted purpose — negotiate these explicitly.
  • AI sub-processors undisclosed. If the vendor's AI features send data to third-party AI APIs, that's a sub-processor handling your PHI — it needs to be in the agreement. (This is one reason to prefer vendors whose AI runs on private infrastructure.)

Making BAAs operational, not just signed

  • Execute before go-live. The BAA must be signed before the vendor touches PHI — that includes implementation, data migration, and pilots.
  • Keep a vendor BAA inventory. Every vendor with PHI access, agreement date, and renewal terms in one list. Review it annually — vendors get acquired and sub-processors change.
  • E-signing is fine. Electronic signatures on BAAs are valid; what's required is the agreement's content and execution, not wet ink. ClaimPaid includes built-in BAA e-signing so the agreement is executed inside the platform before any PHI flows.

Frequently asked questions

Do billing companies need a HIPAA BAA?

Yes. A billing company that creates, receives, maintains, or transmits PHI on your behalf is a HIPAA business associate, and covered entities are required to have a written business associate agreement with every business associate before PHI is shared.

What must a BAA include?

Permitted uses and disclosures of PHI, required safeguards, breach notification duties, subcontractor flow-down requirements, return-or-destruction of PHI at termination, and the business associate's Security Rule obligations.

Is an electronically signed BAA valid?

Yes. HIPAA doesn't require wet ink — electronic signatures are valid. What matters is that the agreement covers the required elements and is executed before the vendor accesses PHI.

Does RCM software need a BAA too?

If the software vendor can access PHI — including cloud-hosted systems and AI features that process claim data — yes, they're a business associate and need a BAA, including disclosure of any AI sub-processors.

What happens if there's no BAA in place?

The covered entity owns the compliance gap. A missing BAA is treated as a basic HIPAA compliance failure in OCR investigations, independent of whether a breach occurred.
