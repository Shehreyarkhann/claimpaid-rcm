Common Billing Modifiers (25, 59, 91, 76) Explained

ClaimPaid · October 23, 2026 · 8 min read
The short answer

Billing modifiers are two-character codes appended to CPT codes to give payers extra context. Modifier 25 marks a significant, separately identifiable E/M service on the same day as a procedure. Modifier 59 flags a distinct procedural service to bypass bundling edits. Modifier 91 indicates a repeat lab test on the same day. Modifier 76 marks a repeat procedure by the same physician on the same day. Misused modifiers are a top trigger for CO-97 bundling denials and audits — documentation must support every one.

Modifiers are the footnotes of medical billing — two characters that change what a CPT code means to the payer. Used correctly, they're the difference between bundled-away revenue and proper payment. Used incorrectly, they're a fast track to denials and audits. These four are the ones billing teams encounter most.

Modifier 25 — Significant, separately identifiable E/M

What it means: on the same day as a procedure, the physician also performed a significant, separately identifiable evaluation and management service.

When to use it: a patient comes in for a scheduled procedure but also presents a new or worsening problem that requires its own workup. The E/M must stand on its own — same-day convenience doesn't qualify.

Watch out: this is one of the most audited modifiers in billing. The documentation must clearly show the separate E/M work; payers routinely downcode or deny E/M lines with modifier 25 that lack it.

CO-97
The bundling denial code most often tied to modifier problems — 'benefit included in another payment.' Correct modifiers are the fix.

Modifier 59 — Distinct procedural service

What it means: the procedure was distinct from other services on the same day — different session, different site, different lesion, or separate encounter.

When to use it: to bypass NCCI bundling edits when two procedures that would normally bundle were genuinely separate. It's the modifier of last resort: if a more specific modifier (like an anatomical modifier) describes the situation, use that instead.

Watch out: modifier 59 use without clear justification is a known audit target. Never append it routinely to get around bundling — every use needs documentation of what made the service distinct.

Modifier 91 — Repeat clinical diagnostic laboratory test

What it means: the same lab test was repeated on the same day to get a subsequent result.

When to use it: serial lab values — a potassium rechecked after treatment, for example. It applies to lab codes, not procedures.

Watch out: don't confuse with modifier 76 (repeat procedure) or 77 (repeat by another physician). And it can't be used when the lab code descriptor already includes multiple tests.

Modifier 76 — Repeat procedure, same physician

What it means: a procedure was repeated on the same day by the same physician.

When to use it: the clinical scenario genuinely required doing the procedure twice — a repeat X-ray because the first was unreadable, for instance. (Modifier 77 is the counterpart for a different physician.)

Watch out: payers may still bundle or reduce the repeat; documentation of medical necessity for the repeat is essential.

How modifier errors become denials

Missing or wrong modifiers surface as CO-97 bundling denials (the payer rolled the line into another payment), CO-16 (missing information), or outright medical-necessity rejections. The pattern to watch: the same code pair denying repeatedly across providers usually means a modifier is missing from the charge workflow, not that individual coders are making mistakes. That's a KPI worth tracking by reason category. Coding-level denials also pair frequently with place-of-service errors — the two most common coding-level denial sources.

Modifier discipline checklist

  • Every modifier on a claim must be supported by documentation a reviewer could follow.
  • Use the most specific modifier available — 59 is last resort, not first instinct.
  • Run NCCI edit checks before submission. A claim scrubber catches bundling conflicts while they're still cheap to fix.
  • Audit modifier 25 and 59 usage periodically — they're the two auditors ask about first.
  • When a payer policy differs from CPT guidance, the payer's written policy wins for that payer. Keep payer-specific modifier rules in your payer reference alongside filing deadlines.

Frequently asked questions

What is modifier 25 used for?

Modifier 25 identifies a significant, separately identifiable E/M service performed on the same day as a procedure. The E/M work must stand on its own in the documentation — it can't just be the pre/post work of the procedure itself. It's one of the most audited modifiers, so documentation matters.

When should I use modifier 59?

Modifier 59 flags a distinct procedural service — different session, site, lesion, or encounter — to bypass NCCI bundling edits when two procedures were genuinely separate. Use a more specific anatomical modifier when one fits; 59 is the modifier of last resort and a known audit target.

What is the difference between modifier 91 and 76?

Modifier 91 is for repeat clinical diagnostic laboratory tests on the same day (serial lab values). Modifier 76 is for a repeat procedure or service by the same physician on the same day. They apply to different code families — don't interchange them.

Why are my claims denying with CO-97?

CO-97 means the payer bundled the line into another payment. The most common fix is a missing or incorrect modifier — the claim didn't tell the payer the service was distinct. Review the code pair against NCCI edits and add the supported modifier.

Can I just add modifier 59 to unbundle everything?

No. Routine modifier 59 use to bypass bundling without documentation support is a well-known audit risk. Every modifier must be backed by documentation showing what made the service distinct.
