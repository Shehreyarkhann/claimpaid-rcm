What Are Crossover Claims? Medicare-Medicaid Billing Explained

ClaimPaid · October 23, 2026 · 7 min read
The short answer

A crossover claim is one that Medicare automatically forwards to a patient's secondary payer — usually Medicaid — after Medicare processes it, through the Coordination of Benefits Agreement (COBA) program. For dual-eligible patients, this means you typically don't bill Medicaid separately; Medicare sends the claim over with its payment information. But crossover doesn't cover every situation: non-covered services, certain claim types, and COBA enrollment gaps still require manual secondary billing.

For patients covered by both Medicare and Medicaid — "dual-eligible" beneficiaries — billing could be a two-step headache on every claim. Crossover is the mechanism that removes the second step: Medicare automatically forwards the processed claim to Medicaid (or another secondary payer), so the biller doesn't have to send it twice. Understanding when crossover works — and when it silently doesn't — prevents some of the most confusing secondary-billing denials in the business.

How crossover works

After Medicare adjudicates a claim, the Coordination of Benefits Agreement (COBA) program transmits the claim data — including what Medicare paid and what remains — directly to the secondary payer. Medicaid then processes its secondary liability and pays accordingly, or determines there's no additional payment due.

For the billing team, the practical effect is simple: for crossover-eligible claims, do not bill Medicaid separately. Submitting a separate secondary claim for a claim that's already crossing over creates duplicates, which create their own denials and reconciliation messes.

When crossover doesn't happen

Crossover is reliable but not universal. Watch for these gaps:

  • Services Medicare doesn't cover: if Medicare denies as non-covered, there's often nothing to cross over — but Medicaid might still cover the service. These need manual secondary billing.
  • COBA enrollment gaps: the secondary payer must participate in COBA for the automatic transfer. If it doesn't, you're billing secondary manually.
  • Certain claim types: some claim formats and settings don't cross over cleanly. Know your state's rules.
  • Eligibility timing: if dual eligibility wasn't active on the date of service, crossover won't fire — and the resulting secondary denial will confuse anyone who assumes it did.

What billers should do

  1. Verify dual eligibility on the date of service — not just "has Medicaid," but active for that date. Our eligibility checklist covers the full verification routine.
  2. Check whether the claim crossed over before billing secondary manually. The remittance advice (ERA or EOB) usually indicates crossover; billing a duplicate secondary claim is a classic self-inflicted denial.
  3. Know your timely filing clocks for both payers. If crossover fails and you discover it late, the secondary payer's filing deadline may already be blown. See timely filing limits by payer.
  4. Understand Medicaid secondary payment rules. Medicaid typically pays only up to its fee schedule — if Medicare already paid more than Medicaid allows, Medicaid pays nothing further. That's normal, not a denial to appeal.

Crossover and coordination of benefits

Crossover is really COB automation — the payer-to-payer handoff that coordination of benefits rules require. When the COB order is wrong in the patient's record (Medicare listed as secondary, for instance), crossover misfires and both payers deny. Getting the coverage order right at registration prevents the whole cascade.

The bottom line

Crossover eliminates duplicate billing work for dual-eligible claims — when it fires. The billing team's job is knowing when it should have fired but didn't, catching that fast, and billing secondary manually before the clock runs out. Trust the automation, but verify it.

Frequently asked questions

What is a crossover claim?

A crossover claim is one that Medicare automatically forwards to the patient's secondary payer (usually Medicaid) after processing, through the COBA program. The biller doesn't submit a separate secondary claim — Medicare sends the claim data, including payment information, directly to the secondary payer.

Do I need to bill Medicaid separately for dual-eligible patients?

Usually not — if the claim is crossover-eligible, Medicare forwards it automatically and billing Medicaid separately creates a duplicate. But when crossover doesn't fire (non-covered services, COBA gaps, eligibility timing issues), manual secondary billing is required.

Why didn't my claim cross over to Medicaid?

Common reasons: the service wasn't covered by Medicare (nothing to forward), the secondary payer doesn't participate in COBA, the claim type doesn't support crossover, or dual eligibility wasn't active on the date of service. Check the remittance advice for crossover indicators.

Does Medicaid always pay the remaining balance after Medicare?

No. Medicaid as secondary typically pays only up to its own fee schedule — if Medicare's payment already meets or exceeds what Medicaid allows, Medicaid pays nothing further. That's the correct outcome, not a denial.

What is COBA in Medicare billing?

COBA (Coordination of Benefits Agreement) is the program through which Medicare electronically transfers processed claim data to secondary payers. It's the mechanism behind crossover claims, replacing the old process of providers billing each payer separately.
