Patient Eligibility Verification Checklist

ClaimPaid · October 23, 2026 · 7 min read
The short answer

Verify these before every visit: (1) coverage is active on the date of service, (2) the payer on file matches the patient's card, (3) coordination of benefits order is correct, (4) the patient's demographic data matches the payer's record, (5) copay, coinsurance, and remaining deductible amounts, (6) whether the service needs prior authorization, (7) whether a referral is required, (8) benefit limits or exclusions for the planned service, (9) workers' comp or auto-liability involvement, and (10) secondary coverage. Eligibility denials are among the most common and most preventable — this checklist is the fix.

Eligibility denials are the most frustrating kind: the care was medically appropriate, the coding was right, the claim was on time — and it still denied because the coverage information was wrong. They're also the most preventable. Nearly every eligibility denial traces back to a verification step that was skipped, rushed, or done once and never repeated. This checklist fixes that.

The 10-point pre-visit checklist

  1. Coverage active on the date of service. Not "has insurance" — active that day. Coverage lapses constantly; verify every visit, not just new patients.
  2. Payer on file matches the patient's card. Patients change plans at open enrollment and forget to mention it. Ask to see the card every time.
  3. Coordination of benefits order is correct. Any other coverage — spouse's plan, former employer, Medicare? Wrong COB order is a top denial cause; see our COB guide.
  4. Demographics match the payer's record. Name spelling, date of birth, member ID — transposed digits deny claims that are otherwise perfect.
  5. Copay, coinsurance, and remaining deductible. Know the patient's financial responsibility before the visit so it can be collected, not billed and chased.
  6. Prior authorization required? Check auth requirements for the planned service before the appointment. Same-day discoveries are schedule disruptions.
  7. Referral required? HMO plans often require PCP referrals. No referral, no payment — regardless of medical necessity.
  8. Benefit limits and exclusions. Visit caps, frequency limits, non-covered services for the patient's plan. Better to know before rendering the service.
  9. Workers' comp or auto liability? If the visit relates to an accident or workplace injury, health insurance may not be the right payer at all.
  10. Secondary coverage identified. Don't stop at the primary — capture the secondary payer now so secondary billing doesn't stall later.

Real-time vs. batch verification

The 270/271 eligibility transaction gives you real-time answers — coverage status, benefits, deductibles — in seconds. If your workflow still relies on batch verification the night before (or a phone call the morning of), you're working with stale data and slow answers. Real-time verification at scheduling and at check-in catches changes that happened between the two.

Making it stick operationally

  • Make it a hard stop. The checklist isn't guidance — the appointment can't be confirmed until it's complete. Build it into the scheduling and check-in workflow, not a separate task.
  • Track eligibility denials as a category. If they persist despite the checklist, the problem is compliance with the process, not the process itself. Our denial KPI guide shows how to measure it.
  • Fix the payer's record, not just yours. When the payer's COB or demographic data is wrong, the patient usually needs to correct it with the payer directly — your record being right isn't enough.
  • Re-verify, don't assume. "Nothing's changed" is an answer you need to hear explicitly, every visit. Coverage changes without patients telling you.

New providers and eligibility

One more timing trap: newly hired providers can't bill until payers finish credentialing them. Credentialing typically takes 90–120 days — if the front desk schedules a new provider's patients before their effective date, those claims will deny regardless of how perfect eligibility verification is.

The payoff

Practices that run this checklist consistently see eligibility denials collapse — because there was never anything medically wrong with those claims, only informationally wrong. It's the highest-ROI ten minutes in the revenue cycle: a few minutes of verification versus weeks of denial rework. Combine it with pre-submission scrubbing and the front end of your revenue cycle becomes genuinely hard to break.

Frequently asked questions

What should I check during eligibility verification?

Confirm active coverage on the date of service, that the payer matches the patient's card, correct COB order, matching demographics, copay/deductible amounts, prior auth and referral requirements, benefit limits, workers' comp involvement, and secondary coverage — ten checks that prevent the most common eligibility denials.

How often should eligibility be verified?

Before every visit — for new and established patients. Coverage lapses and changes happen constantly, and 'nothing changed' needs to be confirmed explicitly each time, not assumed.

What is real-time eligibility verification?

The 270/271 EDI transaction lets you check coverage, benefits, and deductibles in seconds rather than relying on batch checks or phone calls. Run it at scheduling and again at check-in to catch changes between the two.

Why do eligibility denials keep happening if we verify?

Usually one of three things: verification is stale (done once, never repeated), the payer's own record is wrong (COB or demographics — the patient must correct it with the payer), or staff are skipping checklist steps. Track eligibility denials as a KPI category to find which.

What's the difference between eligibility and prior authorization?

Eligibility confirms the patient has active coverage and what the benefits are. Prior authorization is the payer's advance approval for a specific service. A patient can be fully eligible and still need prior auth — check both.
