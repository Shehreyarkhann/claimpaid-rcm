Write-Off vs Adjustment vs Bad Debt: What's the Difference?

ClaimPaid · October 23, 2026 · 7 min read
The short answer

A contractual adjustment is the difference between your billed charge and the payer's contracted allowed amount — it's not real revenue and never was. A write-off is a business decision to forgive a balance you could pursue (small balances, timely-filing losses). Bad debt is patient-owed money you tried to collect and couldn't. The distinction matters for accounting accuracy, tax treatment, and compliance — writing off patient balances too aggressively can violate payer contracts and, for government payers, federal rules.

Billing teams use "write-off" loosely — but in accounting and compliance, contractual adjustments, write-offs, and bad debt are three different things with different rules. Mixing them up corrupts your financial reporting and can create real compliance exposure. Here's the clean distinction.

Contractual adjustments

What it is: the difference between your billed charge and the payer's contracted allowed amount. If you bill $500 and the payer contract allows $320, the $180 difference is a contractual adjustment.

Key point: this was never your revenue. Adjustments are an accounting recognition of the contract terms, not a loss. They should post automatically from the payer's remittance — and a sudden change in adjustment amounts per payer is worth investigating, since it can signal fee schedule changes or payment errors.

A contractual adjustment was never your money. A write-off was your money, and you chose to let it go. Know which one you're looking at.

Write-offs

What it is: a deliberate business decision to forgive a balance that could otherwise be pursued — small-balance write-offs (where collection costs exceed the balance), timely-filing denials that can't be overturned, or administrative write-offs approved by management.

Key point: write-offs require policy and approval authority. Common structures: automatic small-balance thresholds (e.g., balances under a set dollar amount), manager approval above that, and documented reasons for every write-off. Uncontrolled write-offs hide operational failures — a timely-filing write-off is really a process failure wearing an accounting label. See how to fight timely-filing denials before writing them off.

Bad debt

What it is: patient-owed balances (copays, deductibles, coinsurance, self-pay) that were billed, followed up on, and ultimately not collected — then sent to collections or written off as uncollectible.

Key point: bad debt should only exist after a real collection effort: statements sent, calls made, payment plans offered. Balances that go straight to bad debt without follow-up aren't bad debt — they're a broken patient collections process. Track it as a KPI: rising bad debt with steady volume points at front-end collections (see our eligibility checklist — knowing the patient's responsibility before the visit is the best bad-debt prevention).

Compliance guardrails

  • Routine waiver of patient cost-sharing is risky. Regularly writing off copays and deductibles without financial-hardship basis can violate payer contracts and, for federal payers, anti-kickback and false-claims rules. Have a written financial hardship policy and apply it consistently.
  • Medicare bad debt has specific rules. Claiming Medicare bad debt on cost reports requires documented, reasonable collection efforts — the requirements are detailed and audited. Don't improvise; follow the CMS rules.
  • Write-off authority must be documented. Who can write off what amounts, for what reasons, with what documentation. Auditors ask for this.

The bottom line

Keep the three categories separate in your system, your policies, and your reporting. Contractual adjustments are contract math. Write-offs are management decisions that deserve scrutiny — every one is a question about whether the underlying failure is being fixed. Bad debt is the scoreboard for patient collections. When the categories blur, the problems they describe blur with them.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a write-off and a contractual adjustment?

A contractual adjustment is the difference between billed charges and the payer's contracted allowed amount — it was never collectible revenue. A write-off is a deliberate decision to forgive a balance that could have been pursued, like a small balance or an unappealable timely-filing denial.

What counts as bad debt in medical billing?

Patient-owed balances (copays, deductibles, self-pay amounts) that were billed and followed up on through a real collection effort — statements, calls, payment plan offers — but never collected. Balances skipped straight to bad debt without follow-up indicate a broken collections process, not true bad debt.

Can I write off a patient's copay?

Routinely waiving patient cost-sharing without a financial-hardship basis is risky — it can violate payer contracts and, for federal payers, anti-kickback and false-claims rules. Maintain a written financial hardship policy and apply it consistently rather than writing off copays casually.

What are Medicare bad debt rules?

Medicare allows bad debt claims on cost reports only after documented, reasonable collection efforts, under detailed CMS rules that are subject to audit. The requirements are specific — follow them exactly rather than improvising.

Why should write-offs need approval?

Because every write-off is a business decision to forgo revenue, and uncontrolled write-offs hide operational failures. A timely-filing write-off, for example, is really a process failure. Approval policies with documented reasons keep write-offs honest and visible.
