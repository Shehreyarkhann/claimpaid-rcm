How to Appeal an Aetna Timely Filing Denial

ClaimPaid · October 24, 2026 · 7 min read
The short answer

Aetna's timely filing limit is generally 120 days from the date of service (always verify against your specific payer contract). To appeal a CO-29 from Aetna, submit through Availity Essentials with proof the claim was received on time — the 277CA acknowledgment or clearinghouse acceptance report. Appeals without proof of timely submission almost never succeed, so deadline tracking matters more than any appeal tactic.

Aetna CO-29 denials — "time limit for filing expired" — sting because the window is one of the shorter ones in commercial insurance: generally 120 days from the date of service. Miss it, and the denial is usually final. But Aetna denials marked timely-filing aren't always actually late. Clearinghouse hiccups, misrouted claims, and payer-side receipt errors all produce CO-29s on claims that were filed on time. Here's how to tell the difference and what to do about each.

Aetna's timely filing rule

Aetna's standard timely filing limit is 120 days from the date of service — see our payer deadline guide for how that compares (Cigna 180, UHC 90, Anthem 180, Medicare one calendar year). Three things billers get wrong about it:

  • Your contract governs. The 120-day standard can differ in specific provider agreements — verify yours before you assume.
  • Receipt date counts, not submission date. A claim transmitted on day 118 but received by Aetna on day 122 is late.
  • Corrected claims generally must also land inside the window. Fixing a claim doesn't restart the clock — the corrected submission is typically held to the original 120-day deadline.

Step 1: Prove the claim was actually on time

Before writing anything, pull your evidence. If any of these show Aetna received the claim within 120 days of service, you have a winnable appeal:

  • 277CA claim acknowledgment — Aetna's own record of receipt with dates. Strongest evidence.
  • Clearinghouse acceptance report — timestamped proof of transmission and acceptance.
  • 999 functional acknowledgment — proof the EDI file was accepted.
  • Claim status history — if Aetna's system shows the claim at all, note the earliest receipt date on record.

If the evidence shows the claim genuinely arrived after day 120, an appeal will almost certainly fail — put the energy into prevention instead (see below). Our general timely-filing appeal guide covers the evidence packet in detail.

Step 2: File the appeal through Availity Essentials

Aetna routes most provider appeals through Availity Essentials, its provider portal — that's the fastest, most trackable channel. Your submission should include:

  1. The claim number, dates of service, and billed amount.
  2. The CO-29 denial reference from the remittance advice.
  3. The 277CA or clearinghouse report with the receipt date highlighted.
  4. A one-paragraph cover note: the claim was received by Aetna on [date], within the 120-day filing window from the [date] date of service; requesting reprocessing as timely filed.

Keep the argument to one page. The reviewer needs exactly one fact: the receipt date. Longer letters don't improve timely-filing appeals — evidence does. For the general mechanics of denial appeals, see how to appeal a denied claim.

When Aetna grants exceptions

Outside of proof-of-timely-filing, exceptions are narrow and all require documentation:

  • Aetna-caused delays — enrollment or credentialing delays on Aetna's side, or claims Aetna misdirected. Document Aetna's role with dates and reference numbers.
  • Retroactive eligibility — eligibility granted retroactively can justify late filing in some circumstances; state rules and plan terms apply.
  • COB delays — waiting on the primary payer before billing Aetna as secondary. Some secondary payers allow extra time; don't assume Aetna will.
An Aetna timely-filing appeal lives or dies on one thing: proof the claim arrived within 120 days.

Prevention: the only reliable strategy

Because most CO-29s are final, prevention dominates. For Aetna specifically:

  • Submit Aetna claims daily. With a 120-day window, weekly batching burns nearly 6% of your time for nothing.
  • Monitor clearinghouse rejections daily. A rejected claim isn't filed — and the clock runs while it sits in the queue.
  • Diary every held claim with an Aetna filing-deadline date, especially claims held for credentialing or information.
  • Work A/R young. Claims touched at 30 days can't become CO-29s; claims ignored until day 110 can.
Can you bill the patient? For in-network claims, timely-filing denials are generally a provider write-off — billing patients for denials caused by the provider missing a filing deadline is typically prohibited by the payer contract. Check your Aetna agreement before considering it.

Frequently asked questions

What is Aetna's timely filing limit?

Aetna's standard timely filing limit is generally 120 days from the date of service, though specific provider contracts can differ — always verify against your agreement. The deadline is measured by Aetna's receipt date, not your submission date.

How do I appeal an Aetna timely filing denial?

Submit the appeal through Availity Essentials, Aetna's provider portal, attaching proof the claim was received within the filing window — the 277CA acknowledgment or clearinghouse acceptance report with the receipt date highlighted. Appeals without proof of timely submission almost never succeed.

What evidence proves timely filing to Aetna?

The 277CA claim acknowledgment is strongest — it's Aetna's own record of receiving the claim with dates. Clearinghouse acceptance reports and 999 functional acknowledgments also help. Aetna's receipt date is what counts.

Can I bill the patient if Aetna denies for timely filing?

Generally no for in-network claims. Timely-filing denials are typically a provider write-off, and payer contracts usually prohibit balance-billing patients for denials caused by the provider missing a filing deadline.

Do corrected claims get a new filing deadline with Aetna?

No. Corrected claims are generally held to the original timely filing window — fixing a claim does not restart the 120-day clock. Submit corrections as early as possible.
