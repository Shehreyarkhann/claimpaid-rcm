RCM Software Buyer's Guide: What to Look For

ClaimPaid · October 23, 2026 · 9 min read
The short answer

Good RCM software should do seven things well: scrub claims before submission, manage denials through structured appeal workflows, auto-post ERA payments, verify eligibility in real time, report on the KPIs that matter (denial rate, A/R days, net collection rate), integrate with your EHR and clearinghouse, and price transparently. Evaluate vendors against your actual denial data — the best software for you is the one that fixes your specific revenue leaks, not the one with the longest feature list.

Buying revenue cycle software is a high-stakes decision — the system touches every claim, every payment, and every denial your organization handles. But most buying processes go wrong the same way: they compare feature checklists instead of asking which product fixes the buyer's actual revenue leaks. This guide gives you the criteria that matter, in the order that matters.

Start with your denial data, not vendor demos

Before talking to any vendor, pull your own numbers: denial rate by reason category, clean claim rate, days in A/R, and where staff time goes. If 40% of your denials are eligibility-related, eligibility automation matters more than any other feature. If authorizations are the problem, auth tracking is the priority. Our denial KPI guide shows which metrics to pull. The vendor that maps to your data wins — full stop.

The seven criteria that matter

1. Pre-submission claim scrubbing

The highest-ROI capability in RCM software. The scrubber should run payer-specific edits — coding conflicts, missing data, NCCI bundling, authorization checks — and explain why each claim was flagged, in language billers understand. Ask: how many rules, how often updated, can we add our own? (For reference, ClaimPaid's scrubber runs 15 payer-aware rules before every submission — see our claim scrubbing guide for what scrubbers should check.)

2. Denial management workflow

Not just a denial list — a workflow: automatic categorization by reason code, appeal deadline tracking, templated appeal letters, follow-up cadence, and overturn-rate reporting. Ask to see the appeal workflow end to end, not just the dashboard. Vague "denial management" claims without a visible workflow are a red flag.

3. ERA auto-posting and reconciliation

The system should ingest 835s, auto-post payments and adjustments per your rules, and surface exceptions — unposted ERAs, unmapped codes, payment variances. Ask about the exception workflow specifically; auto-posting without exception handling just moves the mess.

4. Real-time eligibility verification

270/271 checks integrated into scheduling and check-in — not a separate portal staff have to remember to open. Given that eligibility failures drive a huge share of preventable denials (see our eligibility checklist), this integration's quality matters enormously.

5. Reporting on the metrics that matter

Denial rate by payer and reason, clean claim rate, A/R aging, net collection rate, overturn rates. If the vendor's standard reports don't include these, you'll be exporting to spreadsheets forever. Ask for a live look at the reports, with real (anonymized) data.

6. Integration: EHR and clearinghouse

How does clinical and scheduling data get in? How do claims get out? Proprietary integrations, HL7/FHIR, clearinghouse partnerships — understand the data path and who maintains it. A beautiful app that can't talk to your EHR is a second system, not a solution.

7. Transparent pricing and support

Per-claim, per-provider, flat monthly, percentage of collections — understand the model and model it against your volume. Percentage-of-collections pricing looks cheap until you grow. And ask about support honestly: who answers, how fast, and do they understand billing or just the software?

Red flags

  • No live product walkthrough of the denial workflow — only slideware.
  • "AI-powered" with nothing to show — ask what the AI actually does, with a demo. (Real AI assistance, like ClaimPaid's coding suggestions from clinical notes, is demonstrable in minutes.)
  • Vague implementation timelines — credentialing, payer enrollment, and EDI setup have real lead times; a vendor that hand-waves them hasn't done this before.
  • No customer references in your specialty. RCM is specialty-specific; a vendor great at primary care may be wrong for surgical specialties.
  • Contracts without exit terms. Know what happens to your data if you leave.

How to run the evaluation

  1. Shortlist 3 vendors max. More than that and nobody gets a serious look.
  2. Give each the same scenario: your top 3 denial reasons, your payer mix, your volume. Ask them to show exactly how their product addresses each.
  3. Talk to references — and ask references about implementation honesty, support responsiveness, and what broke.
  4. Pilot if possible. A 30–60 day pilot on a subset of claims beats a year of regret.
  5. Check the math. Model pricing against your actual volume and project the ROI from your denial data: what would a 2-point clean-claim-rate improvement be worth?

Frequently asked questions

What should I look for in RCM software?

Seven things: pre-submission claim scrubbing, a real denial appeal workflow (not just a list), ERA auto-posting with exception handling, real-time eligibility verification, reporting on denial rate/A/R/clean claim rate, EHR and clearinghouse integration, and transparent pricing. Evaluate against your own denial data, not feature checklists.

How much does RCM software cost?

Pricing models vary: flat monthly per provider, per-claim fees, or percentage of collections. Percentage models look cheap at small volumes but get expensive as you grow. Model each vendor's pricing against your actual claim volume before comparing.

What are red flags when evaluating RCM vendors?

No live walkthrough of the denial workflow, 'AI-powered' claims with nothing demonstrable, vague implementation timelines, no references in your specialty, and contracts without clear exit and data terms.

Should I do a pilot before buying RCM software?

If possible, yes — a 30–60 day pilot on a subset of claims reveals integration friction, workflow fit, and real denial-recovery performance that demos can't show.

How do I calculate RCM software ROI?

Start from your denial data: current denial rate by category, overturn rates, and cost to collect. Model what a realistic improvement (e.g., 2 points of clean claim rate) is worth in recovered revenue and reduced rework labor, then compare against the vendor's total cost.
