How to Write a Prior Authorization Appeal Letter

ClaimPaid · October 23, 2026 · 8 min read
The short answer

A prior authorization appeal letter should: identify the patient, requested service, and denial reference number; state clearly that you're requesting reconsideration; present the clinical case for medical necessity (diagnosis, severity, treatments already tried and failed, supporting evidence); reference applicable clinical guidelines; and request a specific outcome. Attach chart notes, test results, and prior treatment records. If the written appeal fails, request a peer-to-peer review — a physician-to-physician discussion that resolves many denials the paper process won't.

Prior authorization denials feel final. They're not — they're the start of a clinical conversation. The initial denial is usually an administrative or criteria-based decision; the appeal puts the case in front of a medical reviewer who can actually weigh the clinical facts. The letters that win are specific, evidence-backed, and structured for a busy reviewer. Here's how to write one.

Before you write: understand why it was denied

Read the denial notice carefully. Prior auth denials typically fall into three buckets:

  • Administrative: wrong code submitted, incomplete request, or the request went to the wrong department. Fix and resubmit — no clinical argument needed.
  • Criteria not met: the payer's clinical criteria require step therapy or conservative treatment first, and the request didn't document it. This is the most common bucket — and the most winnable with documentation.
  • Not medically necessary (as submitted): the clinical case wasn't made. The documentation didn't show severity, failed alternatives, or guideline support.

Your letter strategy depends on the bucket. Administrative denials need correction, not persuasion. The other two need the full clinical case below.

The template: what every letter needs

Prior Authorization Appeal Letter — Outline

  1. Header: practice letterhead; date; payer appeals address; patient name, DOB, member ID; provider name and NPI; authorization reference/denial number; requested service with CPT/HCPCS code and date(s).
  2. Subject line: "Appeal of prior authorization denial — [reference number] — request for reconsideration."
  3. Opening paragraph: "I am writing to appeal the denial of prior authorization for [service] for [patient], denied on [date] (ref #[number]). I respectfully request reconsideration and approval."
  4. Clinical summary: diagnosis with ICD-10 code, history of present illness, severity and functional impact, relevant comorbidities — concise, factual, 1–2 paragraphs.
  5. Treatment history: conservative treatments already tried, with dates and outcomes ("physical therapy 6 weeks, no improvement; NSAIDs contraindicated due to..."). This is the section that wins criteria-based denials.
  6. Why this service: why the requested service is medically necessary for this patient now — connect the clinical facts to the request explicitly.
  7. Guideline support: cite the relevant clinical practice guideline or peer-reviewed evidence supporting the service for this indication.
  8. Closing request: "I request approval of [service, CPT code] for [dates/units]. I am available for peer-to-peer review at [phone]."
  9. Signature: treating provider's signature, printed name, credentials, date.
  10. Enclosures list: enumerate every attachment (chart notes, imaging, lab results, prior treatment records, guideline excerpts).

What reviewers actually look for

Medical reviewers decide dozens of these daily. The letters that get approved share traits:

  • Failed conservative care, documented: "tried and failed" with dates beats "conservative care inappropriate" every time. If alternatives truly can't be tried, explain exactly why (contraindication, prior adverse reaction) with documentation.
  • Severity quantified: pain scores, functional limitations, failed ADLs — measurable beats adjectives.
  • Guideline alignment: name the guideline (e.g., the relevant specialty society's practice parameter) and quote the applicable recommendation.
  • Brevity: two pages plus attachments. The evidence does the persuading; the letter organizes it.
  • A peer-to-peer offer: always include it. Reviewers often prefer the conversation, and it's your strongest escalation path.

After the letter: the escalation ladder

  1. Written appeal (this letter) — the foundation.
  2. Peer-to-peer review — physician-to-physician call. Prepare a one-page clinical summary; the treating physician should lead.
  3. Second-level appeal — a fresh review, often by a different medical reviewer. Add anything the first denial said was missing.
  4. Expedited appeal — if delay would jeopardize the patient's health, request expedited review (usually decided within 72 hours).
  5. External review — for fully insured plans, the state insurance department's independent review is the final recourse.

Standard vs. expedited appeals

Not all auth appeals run on the same clock. Know which track you're on:

  • Standard appeal: the normal reconsideration process — typically decided within 30–60 days depending on the payer and state. Appropriate when the patient's condition is stable and delay doesn't create risk.
  • Expedited (urgent) appeal: for situations where waiting would jeopardize the patient's life, health, or ability to regain function. Usually decided within 72 hours, sometimes faster. The request must state clearly why the case is urgent — "patient's condition will deteriorate without timely treatment because..." — with clinical support.

Don't file everything as expedited; payers triage, and mislabeled urgency slows the truly urgent cases (including yours, next time). But don't hesitate when the clinical facts support it — that's what the track is for.

Why auth requests get denied administratively

A surprising share of "denials" never reached a medical reviewer. Before writing the clinical case, rule out the administrative causes:

  • The request used an outdated or incorrect CPT/HCPCS code for the service.
  • Clinical documentation was requested and never sent — check fax confirmations and portal uploads.
  • The request went to the wrong payer department or the wrong payer entirely (common with carved-out benefits like behavioral health or radiology).
  • The patient's eligibility changed between the request and the decision.
  • A duplicate request was submitted and the system denied the second as a duplicate.

A five-minute phone call to the payer's auth line often reveals which of these happened — and the fix is a corrected resubmission, not an appeal letter.

Frequently asked questions

What is a prior authorization appeal letter?

A written request asking a payer to reconsider a denied prior authorization. It presents the clinical case for medical necessity — diagnosis, prior treatments tried, supporting evidence, and the requested service — so a reviewer (often a physician) can approve what the initial process denied.

What makes a prior auth appeal successful?

Specific clinical documentation: chart notes showing the diagnosis and severity, records of conservative treatments already tried and failed, relevant lab/imaging results, and references to clinical guidelines supporting the requested service. Vague letters without evidence rarely succeed.

What is a peer-to-peer review?

A phone discussion between the treating physician (or their designee) and the payer's medical reviewer about the denied authorization. It is often the most effective appeal step because it allows real-time clinical discussion rather than paper review.

How long does a prior auth appeal take?

Standard appeals typically take 30 to 60 days depending on the payer and state. Expedited (urgent) appeals for situations where delay would jeopardize the patient's health are usually decided within 72 hours. Check the payer's stated timeframes and follow up.
